# Teach the next idea, not the next digit

PureSudoku should replace its "fill a cell" hint with a five-step ladder that names a technique, points to where it is, shows why it works on the player's own grid, and changes the board only when the player taps Apply. The techniques in the literature fall into four tiers. Basic is singles. Intermediate is intersections and subsets. Advanced is fish, wings, single-digit patterns, coloring and uniqueness. Expert is chains and almost locked sets. About 38 of them are worth teaching. The current engine has the right base (bitmask candidates, a fixed order), but it cannot explain anything. Each elimination technique runs alone on the starting candidates, so steps do not build on each other. The hint returns only a target cell, so the screen cannot show the pattern behind it. Its last resort is brute force, and its difficulty rating measures backtracking, not human technique. For the bedtime side, the evidence is clear: total light matters far more than color tint. So the right design is a true-black screen plus a per-person calibration of dimness, contrast, warmth and text size, not one "Sleep" toggle. Critics have praised exactly one Sudoku app, Good Sudoku. No Sudoku app has won an Apple Design Award. PureSudoku's best path to "top 10" is a combination nobody owns yet: deep teaching, bedtime-first design, and full accessibility for older eyes, with no ads.

## Thirty-eight techniques in four tiers cover what players will meet

The major sources agree on the broad order, even though they rate single steps differently. HoDoKu, the leading open-source desktop solver, ranks over 90 steps from Full House (score 4) to brute force (score 10,000). It groups them into five levels: Easy, Medium, Hard, Unfair and Extreme ([HoDoKu Options.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PseudoFish/Hodoku/master/src/sudoku/Options.java)). Sudoku Explainer (SE), the community's standard rater, gives each step a number from 1.0 to about 11. It rates a puzzle by its **single hardest step**, while HoDoKu **adds up all step scores** ([SukakuExplainer source](https://github.com/SudokuMonster/SukakuExplainer); [HoDoKu docs](https://hodoku.sourceforge.net/en/docs_cre.php)). Sudopedia gives the community category names but no levels ([Sudopedia](http://sudopedia.enjoysudoku.com/Solving_Technique.html)). The sources disagree mostly in the middle. SE rates a hidden single in a box (1.2) as easier than a naked single (2.3), and HoDoKu does the reverse. SE puts X-Wing (3.2) before hidden pair (3.4). SE has no rules at all for Skyscraper, W-Wing or coloring, so it sees them only as chains rated 6.5 and up. As a result, SE overstates the difficulty of puzzles that people solve with those patterns.

A few terms make the tiers readable. A *candidate* is a pencil mark. A *house* is a row, column or box. A *strong link* means a digit has exactly two places in a house, so if one is false the other is true. A *bivalue cell* has exactly two candidates. An *almost locked set* (ALS) is N cells that hold N+1 candidates. These concepts gate whole families of techniques, so the curriculum should teach each as its own short lesson.

| Tier | Techniques, in teaching order | Current PureSudoku engine |
|---|---|---|
| **Basic** | Full House; Hidden Single (box); Hidden Single (row/column); Naked Single; *concept: candidates* | All present |
| **Intermediate** | Pointing; Claiming; Naked Pair; Hidden Pair; Locked Pair/Triple; Naked Triple; Hidden Triple; Naked/Hidden Quad | Pointing, claiming, naked sets only. No hidden subsets. |
| **Advanced** | *concept: strong link*; X-Wing; Skyscraper; 2-String Kite; Empty Rectangle; Swordfish (Jellyfish as a note); *concept: bivalue cell*; XY-Wing; XYZ-Wing; W-Wing; Simple Coloring; Unique Rectangle 1; UR types 2 and 4; BUG+1 | X-Wing, Y-Wing, Swordfish only |
| **Expert** | Finned/Sashimi X-Wing; X-Chain; XY-Chain; AIC (alternating inference chain); Grouped AIC; Multi-Coloring or 3D Medusa; Finned Swordfish; *concept: ALS*; ALS-XZ; ALS-XY-Wing; Sue de Coq; Death Blossom (optional); Forcing Chains as the last logical step | XY-Chain only (and over-restricted) |
| **Out of scope** | Franken, Mutant and Kraken fish; Exocet; templates; Nishio; Bowman's Bingo; brute force | Bowman's Bingo present; Exocet listed in HINT_STRATEGIES.md |

This curriculum is a synthesis. No tested four-tier curriculum was found in the literature. It follows HoDoKu's level split and its "Simple Sudoku Technique Set", which forum solvers use to reduce a puzzle "to the point where the fun begins" ([HoDoKu docs](https://hodoku.sourceforge.net/en/docs_cre.php)).

Players need fewer techniques than the catalog suggests. In one site's self-reported study of its own generator, singles alone finished **192 of 200 Easy** boards and **177 of 200 Hard** boards. The other Hard boards needed only pointing or claiming. On 5,000 "Evil" boards, the most-needed advanced techniques were ALS (2,761 boards), W-Wing (1,769) and XY-Wing (1,743). Jellyfish was needed in only 7 ([sudoku-online-puzzles.com](https://sudoku-online-puzzles.com/blog/sudoku-techniques/)). Of 345 Finned X-Wing moves, only 12 had no simpler alternative. Sue de Coq was never the only way forward. The data comes from one non-peer-reviewed source, so treat it as a rough guide. It still supports teaching W-Wing and XY-Wing early and treating Jellyfish, Remote Pairs and Sue de Coq as footnotes. Research by Pelánek adds a second point: the number of available moves at each step affects difficulty as much as the hardest technique does. A grade that counted only technique names would miss that. His human-model metric correlates **r = 0.95** with real solve times. The number of givens correlates only about 0.25 ([Pelánek, arXiv 1403.7373](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1403.7373)).

One family needs a guard. Unique Rectangles, BUG+1, Hidden and Avoidable Rectangles all assume the puzzle has exactly one solution. Sudopedia notes that "nothing in the rules ... entitles a solver to assume" this ([Sudopedia: Uniqueness Controversy](http://sudopedia.enjoysudoku.com/Uniqueness_Controversy.html)). This affects PureSudoku directly. BYOS.md says imported puzzles need only be **solvable, not unique**. On a puzzle with two solutions, a Unique Rectangle hint can remove the solution the app has stored. So the engine must count solutions (stopping at two) on import. If the count is not exactly one, it must turn off this family. The same check also shows that Check and Reveal have no single correct answer on such a puzzle.

## The current engine finds placements but cannot show its reasoning

The mature solvers share one structure, and PureSudoku should copy that structure, not the code. HoDoKu keeps a 9-bit candidate mask per cell and one 81-cell set per digit. It keeps a precomputed table of each cell's 20 peers. It runs one detector per technique family in a fixed order, cheapest first. After any step, placement or elimination, it applies the step and **restarts from the top** ([hodoku-py architecture notes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/alexdej/hodoku-py/main/docs/ARCHITECTURE.md); [HoDoKu solver docs](https://hodoku.sourceforge.net/en/docs_solv.php)). Each step becomes a record. The record holds the technique, its placements, the candidates it deletes, the base and cover houses, the pattern cells and fins, the chains, and the ALSs. A screen needs exactly this data to draw a hint. HoDoKu is GPLv3 and SE is LGPL, so treat both as reference tools for checking ratings, not as code to ship in an App Store binary.

A reading of `PureSudoku/Engine/SudokuHintEngine.swift` against that model finds six specific gaps. First, **eliminations do not stack**. Each technique starts again from the original candidates, so a pointing step that enables a naked pair produces no hint at the right level. Second, each `apply…` function removes *every* instance on the board at once. It then returns a `GeneratorHint` that holds only a target cell, a digit and a string. The screen therefore cannot highlight the X-Wing rows or the Y-Wing pivot. Third, the XY-Chain search records an elimination only when the chain's end cell sees its start cell. A valid chain removes the digit from *any* cell that sees both ends, so most XY-Chains are missed. Fourth, "Bowman's Bingo" runs the full backtracking solver on trial values and can return `nil`. The player then gets no hint at all. Fifth, HINT_STRATEGIES.md lists Unique Rectangles, Medusa, Exocet and Sue de Coq, but none of them exist in code. It also leaves out hidden subsets, Skyscraper, W-Wing and XYZ-Wing, which are easier and far more common. Sixth, `ratePuzzle` in `SudokuGeneratorCore.swift` averages guess depth over four random runs. Its internal deduction uses singles only. So the rating cannot tell a pairs puzzle from an X-Wing puzzle, and its value changes between runs. The generator then returns the closest of 25 tries, so a "Hard" request can silently return a Medium puzzle (`SudokuGeneratorService.swift`).

The fix is a new logical solver beside the current one, built in this order:

| Change | What it does |
|---|---|
| `TechniqueStep` value type (`Codable`) | Holds technique, placements, eliminations, houses with a role (base/cover), pattern cells with a role (defining, fin, pivot, pincer), links (strong/weak), and an explanation key with arguments. Text comes from one template per technique, so it is testable and localizable. |
| `LogicalSolver.nextStep(board) -> TechniqueStep?` | Tries detectors in curriculum order and returns the first step found. Callers apply it and call again. This one loop drives hints, grading and drills. |
| Bitset board | `[UInt16]` cell masks, per-digit cell sets as `SIMD2<UInt64>`, static peer and house tables, per-house digit masks. Replaces `Set<Int>` peers and the array-building `combinations()` in hot loops. |
| Deterministic tie-break | Within a technique, sort by (fewest pattern cells, most eliminations, lowest cell index). Prefer instances near the selected or last-filled cell. Never rely on Swift `Set` order, which changes between runs. |
| Link graph for chains | One graph over at most 729 (cell, digit) nodes with strong and weak edges. Breadth-first search returns the shortest chain first, which is also the clearest hint. Fixes the XY-Chain bug as a side effect. |
| Honest last resort | Replace Bowman's Bingo with: "I can't find a step I can explain. Reveal this cell?" The stored solution makes this exact. |
| Logical grade, stored | At generation time, store the hardest tier (SE-style), the summed score (HoDoKu-style), options per step over the first 20–30 steps (Pelánek), and the set of techniques used. Reject a puzzle that misses the requested level. Never fall back silently. |
| Solution counter on import | Stop at 2 solutions. If the puzzle is not unique, set a flag that turns off the uniqueness family and marks the puzzle in the UI. |
| Correctness oracle | In DEBUG builds and tests, assert that every placement equals the solution and no elimination removes it. Run the solver in CI over a few thousand public-domain puzzles. |

Two input rules keep hints honest. The hint must come from the true candidates: givens plus *correct* entries, with candidates derived from them. `GameViewModel.requestHint()` already refuses to hint while a wrong entry exists, which is correct. The player's own notes are compared separately. If a note set is missing the correct digit, that error is the more useful hint. If the step uses an elimination the player has not made yet, the hint should say which marks to remove first.

Performance is not a concern up to the Expert tier. A fast brute-force solver handles 17-clue puzzles in about 2.7 µs on a desktop CPU ([tdoku](https://github.com/t-dillon/tdoku)). The estimates for a bitset ladder on a phone are unmeasured. They are under 0.1 ms for singles, under 1 ms for subsets, fish and wings, and a few milliseconds for chain search. That fits easily in a 50 ms budget. Add an XCTest `measure {}` benchmark before committing to the Expert tier. Forcing nets and mutant fish are the only expensive parts. HoDoKu's mutant fish search alone can try about 134,000 base combinations, which is one more reason to exclude them.

Practice drills need no new generator. The Sudoku Exchange bank holds several hundred thousand unique puzzles, rated with SE and dedicated to the public domain. Each record is 100 bytes, so 20–50k puzzles take 2–5 MB before compression ([sudoku-exchange-puzzle-bank](https://github.com/grantm/sudoku-exchange-puzzle-bank)). At build time, run PureSudoku's own solver over a slice. Ship an index that maps each technique to (puzzle id, step number). At run time, replay the solve to that step and show the board there. HoDoKu's learning mode works the same way ([HoDoKu docs](https://hodoku.sourceforge.net/en/docs_cre.php)). The index must come from PureSudoku's own solver and order. HoDoKu warns that reordering techniques changes which steps appear, so an SE label alone would break drills.

## A five-rung hint ladder that fades as skill grows

Players praise apps that read their own board, name the technique and tie practice to it. Good Sudoku's solver "reads your answers and notes" and suggests "the next technique you need". It also lists the techniques each difficulty level requires and tracks which ones you have learned ([App Store: Good Sudoku](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/good-sudoku-by-zach-gage/id1489118195)). HoDoKu offers a vague hint (the name only), a concrete hint, and a full step with Execute or Cancel ([HoDoKu docs](https://hodoku.sourceforge.net/en/docs_play.php)). Hintoku uses three taps: the difficulty, then the name plus a tip, then a visual guide on the player's own puzzle ([App Store: Hintoku](https://apps.apple.com/app/6744828400)). The clearest anti-pattern comes from Sudoku.com reviews: a HINT button that "just reveals the square" ([JustUseApp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/1193508329/sudoku-com-sudoku-puzzle/reviews)). Users also complain when the explanation does not last. Good Sudoku's "Show Me" screen vanishes in about a second, and reviewers ask for a before/after toggle.

Learning research backs a graduated ladder, but it also warns that people skip to the answer. In one tutoring study, students misused hints on **36% of actions** (14% if rapid repeats count once). This figure comes from secondary summaries ([LearnLab](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Help_abuse)). The final answer-giving hint is not always bad: when it is read, it works as a worked example ([Shih, Koedinger and Scheines 2008](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/docs/scheines/Shih_Koedinger_Scheines_2008_EDM.pdf)). Help should also fade as skill grows. In two experiments, **adaptive fading beat fixed fading, which beat no examples**. Full worked examples can hurt experts, an effect called "expertise reversal" ([Salden et al., via CDLib](https://repositories.cdlib.org/content/qt27j4x4r8/qt27j4x4r8.pdf?t=op2jmj)). Producing an answer yourself improves memory (effect size about 0.40), and so does testing yourself (about 0.50) ([Frontiers in Psychology 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01483/pdf)). No study covers hint ladders in puzzle games, so all of this transfers from math and geometry tutors.

| Rung | What the player sees | Board changes? |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Check** (automatic, first) | If a wrong digit or a note set missing the correct digit exists: "There's a mistake on the board." Further taps narrow it to the house, then the cell. | No |
| **1. Name** | "A **Hidden Single** is available" with the tier badge and a one-line definition. A first-time technique adds an offer: "New technique, see a 30-second lesson?" | No |
| **2. Where** | The house or houses to look at get a soft outline: "Look at box 5." | No |
| **3. Why** | Pattern cells are outlined and the key digit is ringed. Chains show solid strong links and dashed weak links. Complex steps split into numbered sub-steps with Back and Next. | No |
| **4. Result** | Eliminated candidates are struck through, or the placed digit appears as a ghost. A Before/After toggle stays until the player taps Done. **Apply** makes the change and can be undone. | Only on Apply |

Each rung is one tap. Rung 0 has no skip-to-answer, and rung 4 asks once, "Try it yourself first?", which the player can turn off. **Reveal Cell stays a separate, honestly named action.** Fading works per technique. Once a player has found a technique unaided in three separate puzzles on different days, its hints open at rung 1 and stop there unless the player taps again. A technique the player has never seen opens straight to rung 3, which acts as a worked example. Hint depth per technique, not hint count, is the skill signal. The app tracks a per-technique strength value that slowly decays, like Duolingo's half-life model (recall = 2^(−days/half-life)). Duolingo reported that this model nearly halved the prediction error of the simpler system it replaced ([Settles and Meeder 2016](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/P16-1174.pdf)). When a technique's strength falls, the app offers an optional review drill, with no streak alarms.

The curriculum lives in a quiet "Learn" screen with the four tiers. Each technique gets one lesson built on a single example, never a catalog of variants. Zach Gage, Good Sudoku's designer, criticized guides that show "eight ways this can show up", which forces players to cross-reference ([hey.gg interview](https://www.hey.gg/blog/zachgage)). Each lesson ends with three drills from the bundled index. Difficulty buttons list the techniques they need, such as "Medium: singles, pointing, pairs". The player meets a new lesson the first time a puzzle needs it.

The panel itself must fit bedtime play. It is a bottom sheet over the number pad, with Back, Next, Apply and Done in thumb reach and the grid fully visible above. Highlights fade in slowly and never blink. The panel never advances on its own. Each step shows one or two short lines, with "More" for detail. Cell names read "row 3, column 5" for beginners and r3c5 once the player turns that on. Roles use shape and weight, not color alone: outlines for pattern cells, rings for the key digit, strike-through for eliminations. These cues survive dimming, warm palettes, color blindness and aging eyes. One existing setting conflicts with this design. `autoFillHints` defaults to `true` in `Settings.swift`, so today a hint fills the cell and marks the game as revealed. The new ladder makes this setting unnecessary.

## Darkness protects sleep better than warmth, so calibrate instead of toggling

The sleep evidence points to brightness, not tint. In a small inpatient study (n=12), four hours of iPad reading each night for five nights delayed the body clock and suppressed melatonin compared with a printed book ([Chang et al. 2015, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25535358/)). That is a far heavier dose than a 15-minute puzzle. Warm tint alone did little. Apple's Night Shift showed no sleep benefit in 167 young adults ([Duraccio et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/doc/zeo/2021-duraccio.pdf)). An expert consensus found that cutting short-wavelength light removes only about **50%** of its effect on the body clock. It recommends dimming as well, and **≤1 lux melanopic light** (the light that affects the body clock) in the sleep room ([Brown et al. 2022, PLOS Biology](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571)). People also differ hugely. The light level that halved melatonin ranged **from 6 to 350 lux** across 55 young adults ([Phillips et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6575863)). The design follows directly. Use true black so OLED pixels are off. Dim the lit pixels. Treat warmth as a comfort setting, never a sleep claim. Tune the settings per person.

Age changes the target. A typical 60-year-old's retina gets about **one third** of the light a 20-year-old's gets, because of smaller pupils and a yellowing lens ([Lighting Research Center](https://www.lrc.rpi.edu/programs/lightHealth/AARP/healthcare/lightingOlderAdults/agingEye.asp)). Contrast sensitivity starts to fall around 40–50 and falls further in dim light ([Scharff summary](https://laurenscharff.com/research/agecontrast.html)). Faint text slows older readers more than younger ones ([PMC6233613](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6233613/)). A 30-year-old can read a very dim, low-contrast amber grid. A 60-year-old needs the same dark room but a brighter digit, stronger contrast, bolder weights and larger candidate marks. The 60-year-old should also not have to tell blue from grey. So **dimness and contrast must be separate controls.** The current design cannot do this. `SleepBrightness` has three fixed overlay steps (0, 0.25, 0.45 black opacity), and a uniform overlay dims every element by the same factor.

The proposed calibration takes about a minute, once, in a dark room, and can be re-run at any time. The player first sets text size on a sample grid with candidates, as a scale on top of Dynamic Type. Next comes dimness: "Slide down until digits are just comfortable, then go one step up." Then contrast, which separates digits, candidates and grid lines and sets a floor for candidate marks. Then warmth, from neutral through amber to an orange-red. A deep red would look nearly black to some color-blind users ([Mantiuk et al. 2009](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/imager/tr/2009/Mantiuk_Night_Viewing)). Last is an opt-in to drop screen brightness to the system floor while the game is open. Apps can set `UIScreen.brightness` only in the foreground, and iOS restores it later ([Apple QA1751](https://developer.apple.com/library/archive/qa/qa1751/_index.html)). The app stores the result as two or three named profiles, such as "Reading in bed" and "Night wake-up". Profiles also let two people share one phone. The app computes palette colors from these values instead of laying a black overlay on top.

iOS sets hard limits. An app cannot go below the system brightness floor, cannot read ambient light as a lux value, and cannot turn on Reduce White Point. Apple's own accessibility features go far lower than any app can. On iPhone 17, Reduce White Point plus the Zoom Low Light filter measured about **0.004 nits**, against roughly 0.5 nits on competing phones ([LTT Labs](https://www.lttlabs.com/articles/2026/04/16/phone-minimum-display-brightness)). The calibration should end with a one-line tip that shows how to add these to the triple-click shortcut. The app should also read Bold Text and Increase Contrast and raise its floors when they are on. Reduce White Point is believed to be readable through an API, but this is unverified. If it is on, the app should keep its own dimming from stacking into illegible text. Auto-switching into bedtime should run on a schedule, not on sensors.

Partner disturbance has no published research, so the rules follow from how light works. On OLED, spill scales with lit area. A dark grid lights only a few percent of pixels. The real danger is a sudden white sheet. Bedtime Mode should therefore force dark appearance, use a black launch screen, avoid system views it cannot theme, and replace completion effects with a slow fade. Error marks must not rely on red, because red disappears in a red-amber palette. They should use a corner marker or underline plus a brightness change. LCD iPhones leak backlight, so system brightness matters more there.

## The award case is an inclusivity story, not "another Sudoku"

The critical bar comes from one app. John Gruber wrote that among "hundreds, maybe thousands of Sudoku video games ... none of them are great (and most of them stink)". He called Good Sudoku "Sudoku with great design, consideration, and craftsmanship" at a $4 one-time unlock ([Daring Fireball](https://daringfireball.net/2020/07/good_sudoku_by_zach_gage)). MacStories praised its Improve section and its tools that "remove the most frustrating aspects", noting they feel "a little like cheating" ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/game-day-good-sudoku/)). Vice's reviewer cut Expert times from 15 to under 10 minutes ([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/good-sudoku-is-making-me-good-at-sudoku)). Pen-and-paper solvers found it too easy, and no Sudoku app has won an Apple Design Award ([Apple Design Awards](https://developer.apple.com/design/awards/)). Ads are the most documented user complaint against mass-market leaders ([Sensor Tower](https://app.sensortower.com/api/ios/apps/366247306?country=US)). A plain no-ads app from 2009 still holds **4.8 stars from 34,000 ratings** ([AppGoblin](https://appgoblin.info/apps/329981776)).

Three positions are open, and PureSudoku can hold all three at once. The first is a teacher that reaches past Good Sudoku's ceiling into chains and ALS, with no ads and no hint paywall. The second is bedtime-first design as a feature, which no reviewed competitor owns. The third is mainstream polish plus full accessibility. Today, blind players use niche VoiceOver apps ([AppleVis: SudokuBlind](https://applevis.com/apps/ios/games/sudokublind)). A small puzzle game, Art of Fauna, won the Inclusivity award in 2025 ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/news/2025-apple-design-awards-winners-and-finalists-announced/)). That category, "a great experience for all by reflecting a variety of backgrounds, abilities", fits per-age calibration and VoiceOver grid play better than Innovation does. Innovation demands novelty "in their genre". The modern input baseline is also clear. It means one-tap pen/pencil switching, optional auto-notes, digit focus with peer highlights, unlimited undo, state that survives leaving the app, and a dark theme without white flashes. Every assist needs a switch that really works. A Good Sudoku reviewer complained that an assist ignored its off setting. The business model critics accept is free to try plus one unlock, or paid upfront. PureSudoku's offline, no-backend rule fits that model well.

| **12.7** Bedtime Mode | Apply the chosen bedtime profile. Force dark appearance and a black launch screen. Avoid unthemed system sheets. |

HINT_STRATEGIES.md should be rewritten to match. It should drop Exocet and Bowman's Bingo and stop claiming hints are "placement-only". BYOS.md's line "Hints work as normal" also needs the uniqueness caveat.

## Conclusion

The research turns "add better hints" into a smaller but more exact task. The hard part is not the technique count. About 20 detectors cover nearly everything a player will meet before the Expert tier. The real foundation is an engine that applies one step at a time and describes it as data, because the ladder, the grading, the drills and the Learn screen all read from that same step record. Once that exists, Pelánek's finding implies something other apps rarely use: PureSudoku can grade puzzles by the experience of solving them, not just by their hardest trick. A good Easy puzzle then means "many easy moves at once", not just "singles only".

The bedtime evidence changes the product story too. Warm tint is the feature every dark-mode app copies, and it is the part with the weakest evidence. Darkness, per-person calibration and color-free cues are well supported, and they are what a 60-year-old in bed actually needs. Several questions remain open. No study measures light reaching a sleeping partner. The phone performance figures are estimates. The four-tier order has not been tested on learners. Short tests in a real dark room with players near 30 and near 60 would settle the calibration defaults faster than more reading would.
